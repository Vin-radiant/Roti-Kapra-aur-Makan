How inflation is squeezing Islamabad’s middle class

Official figures describe the average household. We asked 78 people how they are actually coping.

Background

Inflation is a tax levied without consent, hitting the pockets of certain individuals hardest.

Those with hedging assets and adaptive incomes adjust.

The middle-class, with most of their savings in financial products eroded by inflation, and inflexible incomes, suffer with rent, education, utility bills and food prices going up.

Government assistance may help, but support is not universal, and may not fully offset rising living costs.

Consideration and intervention in policymaking remains scarce on this topic.

How is the middle class defined?

There is still uncertainty regarding the definition of what constitutes the middle class, despite its widespread usage across economics and social sciences.

Since the term has multiple uses, researchers rely on various methods based on what needs to be assessed - income inequality, spending behavior, socio-economic position, and many others.

As Banerjee & Duflo (2008) show in their analysis of the middle classes of developing countries, it relies heavily on consumption-based cuts in incomes. Herein lies the emphasis on the per capita spending capacity on daily necessities of individuals, taking purchasing power into account as well. The findings reveal that middle classes in developing nations cannot be analyzed using the framework of middle-income groups in developed economies.

Alternatively, defining the middle class by relative income entails analyzing households according to their position within the distribution of national incomes.

Essentially, the middle class is defined concerning other households' income levels within the same economy.

Income-based approaches involve determining the level of income or consumption of households within specific monetary thresholds. Meanwhile, asset-based approaches entail assessing households' socioeconomic standing using variables such as homeownership, possession of durables, and accessibility to facilities.

While these approaches exhibit various advantages and drawbacks, the income-based approach proves relatively easy to implement despite its sensitivity towards inflation and volatility regarding income. On the other hand, relative income approaches depict the standing of households according to income distribution yet fail to provide adequate insights regarding the purchasing power. Meanwhile, although asset-based approaches measure the socioeconomic standing of households across extended periods, they often overlook any financial hardships arising due to escalating prices.

Therefore, the middle-class category must not be seen as homogenous. Middle-class households could prove highly variable when considering factors such as the reliability of their incomes, commitments concerning expenditure, accumulation of wealth, and their resilience to withstand financial shocks.

For purposes of current research, middle-class households will be defined economically based on household incomes, expenditures, and the capacity to sustain a decent lifestyle. Such a definition is fitting for the investigation of financial impacts resulting from inflation.

One key contribution to the literature on Pakistan’s middle class is made by Nayab (2011), who looks into the challenges faced by researchers when they seek to identify and measure the middle class in Pakistan. According to the author, the problem lies in the assumption that the middle class and middle income are interchangeable terms, even though the concept of social class takes into account various factors besides income alone. Thus, the paper proposes employing a multidimensional methodology including income, occupation, educational attainment, homeownership, and lifestyle patterns. The use of the 2007-2008 Pakistan Social and Living Standards Measurement Survey (PSLM) enabled the author to conclude that the broad definition covers 35 percent of Pakistan’s population. Furthermore, it has been stressed that multidimensional measures could prove resistant to rapid fluctuations in household income. This conclusion is vital for my research, as inflation causes the actual income level to drop despite occupation, education, and property ownership remaining unchanged. This means that a family could fall under the same socioeconomic class even though they are suffering financially.

As defined by Rehman, Zafar, and Amir-ud-Din (2021), the study further explores the middle class in Pakistan in terms of magnitude, demographics, and lifestyle based on the Pakistan Social and Living Standards Measurement (PSLM) surveys from 2004 to 2014. According to their research methodology, the concept of the middle class is identified using three definitions, namely absolute income, relative income, and asset-based measures. The results indicate that the estimation of the middle class varies significantly across different measures adopted by them.

The middle class in Pakistan is associated with education levels, urban settings, and industrial occupation. Such a conclusion proves that defining the middle class cannot be regarded merely as a technical aspect of the research but rather has implications on household identification as well as conclusions drawn by researchers.

The numbers right now

Consumer price inflation reached nearly 30% in late 2023 and fell to about 1.5% in February 2025 (PBS monthly CPI releases). The relief did not last. Average inflation for FY2025–26 was 7.05%, up from 4.49% the year before, and monthly readings were back in double digits by mid-2026 (11.7% in May, 11.1% in June).

The State Bank of Pakistan raised its policy rate by 100 basis points to 11.5% in 2026 and held it at its 14 September meeting, citing the Middle East conflict’s pressure on commodity prices and supply chains.

The latest PBS data show headline CPI at 10.26% in September 2026 against 5.76% a year earlier, and a first-quarter FY2026–27 average of 10.20% against 4.30%. July was 9.2% and August 11.15%. Wholesale inflation was 13.33%, which suggests upstream costs may still be working their way through to shoppers, and core inflation was about 8.6%, so the pressure is not confined to a few goods. Petrol was 39% more expensive than a year earlier.

These are national averages. They say nothing about which groups carry the burden, which is the gap this project looks at in Islamabad.

Source: Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, September 2026 CPI release.

Why Islamabad?

Islamabad is different and deserves its very own study. Being the capital city of Pakistan means there is a large number of government servants, foreign diplomatic missions and their respective supporting staff, along with highly-paid professional employees. The urban planning and development of Islamabad comes with a significantly higher cost of living, especially when compared with the rest of Pakistan. While some families may belong to the middle-class bracket somewhere else, the same doesn’t apply to Islamabad as far as income goes.

The theory: how inflation hits households

Inflation has been talked about extensively as a macroeconomic concept impacting the economy. However, the economic literature has proven otherwise and revealed how unequal the impact is upon the distribution among households.

Exploring the topic of inflation disparity, Hobijn & Lagakos (2005) stress how crucial understanding consumption habit differences is when analyzing the impact of inflation among the population groups. Price levels increase differently for distinct products and services; moreover, different spending amounts are dedicated to various products and services according to every household’s budget allocation. Therefore, a rising level of costs associated with some products and/or services may impact some households disproportionately compared to others.

This becomes particularly true when prices for fundamental items, such as groceries, energy supplies, accommodation fees, and public transportations, rise faster than those for others.

Households that spend considerable portions of their budget purchasing those goods will feel the pinch harder despite experiencing the same inflation rates as all other households within the country.

From the data provided by the World Bank (2022), there seem to be three major ways inflation impacts household welfare –

  1. the decline in real purchasing power
  2. loss of real incomes due to failure of wages
  3. transfers to adjust themselves to increasing prices, and redistributions through changes in real asset positions

Depending upon household circumstances like consumption profile, labor market situation, and financial position, the relative significance of any particular way differs.

While low-income households remain vulnerable owing to greater weightage of necessity-related expenses, even middle-income households might be experiencing severe financial stress due to inflation. For middle-income households too, vulnerability comes from increases in necessity-related expenses, inflexibility of wages, continuous monetary commitments, and lack of access to selective support programs.

However, merely looking at nationwide CPI statistics is insufficient while evaluating the impact of inflation on inequality in distribution of welfare benefits. What’s needed is an individual household assessment to determine who is actually suffering financially because of inflation.

Inflation comes with many effects, among which one of the most immediate consequences would be a drop in purchasing power whenever the nominal income doesn't rise along with the rising prices. Nominal income is basically the monetary income earned by individuals and households, and real income refers to the purchasing power of that income adjusted according to the fluctuations in the price level.

Suppose a family earns the same monthly salary every month while the prices of groceries, electricity bills, commute expenses, and tuition fees rise. Their nominal income will remain unchanged; however, their purchasing capacity with the same income becomes lower, resulting in dropping real income levels. Such distinctions are highly relevant for salaried families, where salary revisions are done after long intervals.

Whenever these families fall short of keeping up with inflation rates through salary revisions, they face decreased standards of living even with the same nominal income.

The Pakistan Development Update (2022) of the World Bank stresses the effects of inflation on decreasing household welfare by lowering real purchasing power and inadequately adjusting wage levels and transfer payments. In addition, looking into individual household-level disparities becomes necessary rather than focusing on aggregate-level inflation metrics alone.

However, inflation has another side effect on household financial well-being by influencing savings. Whenever the returns on saving in financial instruments drop lower than inflation rates, purchasing power starts diminishing. It makes households weaker to deal with unforeseen circumstances and maintain their standard of living.

Nevertheless, the correlation between savings and inflation is inconsistent because households benefitting from inflation-adjusted earnings, financial investments yielding high returns, or large holdings in physical assets might be well equipped to preserve their purchasing powers.

On the contrary, households relying on stagnant salary structures and poor-performing financial instruments face considerable challenges.

All such factors imply that any assessment of middle income households' welfare under inflation needs careful examination in terms of income and expenditure measures. It means that the financial health of households relies not merely upon their income level but rather on how far that income satisfies their needs.

What households told us

We surveyed 78 people. Respondents could pick up to two stressors and tick every adjustment that applied, so percentages add to more than 100%.

Which expenses hurt most?

Petrol and commuting led (49 of 78). Because these costs are hard to avoid or substitute, price hikes act like a flat tax on disposable income.

How are households adapting?

Delaying major purchases and cutting AC use led the list, alongside changing transport habits and switching from imported or branded groceries to local ones.

How is the monthly budget balanced?

Over half cut non-essential spending completely and a third seek extra income. Only 9.1% draw on savings, which may point to thin buffers or caution amid uncertainty.

Conclusions

  • Stress sits in price-inelastic essentials, led by petrol and commuting and then electricity and gas.
  • Discretionary spending and brand loyalty give way first: 34 households delayed major purchases and 28 switched to local groceries.
  • Budgets adjust before savings do: 51.9% cut spending while 9.1% use savings.
  • Some are working more: 33.8% are seeking additional income.
  • Energy prices are a key policy lever for household welfare.

Limitations

This is a small, self-selected sample of 78 responses. It is not representative of Islamabad as a whole, and respondents were not screened by income or class. Treat the findings as indicative, not definitive.
