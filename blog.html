Investing guide

A beginner’s guide to investing in Pakistan

What inflation does to idle savings, what you can invest in, and how to get started, including halal options.

Please note: this guide is educational, not financial advice. Account limits, fees and Shariah rulings vary, so check current details with a licensed broker and a qualified scholar before investing.

Whether it’s another source of income or a stimulating activity, investing is an excellent way to put your finances to use rather than let them lose value in a bank account. But what is investing, how do you do it, and why exactly should you invest instead of doing something else? Let’s answer those questions.

What is Inflation?

Inflation is a word we’ve all heard before, but what does it actually mean? We tend to associate the word with “mehngai” but there is more to it. Simply put, inflation is the rise in general prices in an economy over time.

Types of inflation are important to understand since they can influence the stocks you buy.

  • Demand-Pull Inflation occurs when the customers’ demand for a good or service exceeds what the economy can supply at a point in time.
  • When the cost of the raw materials required to make something increases, naturally, the cost of that something must as well. This phenomenon is called cost-push inflation.
  • Built-in or Wage-Price Spiral inflation, despite its intimidating name, is similar to cost-push inflation, where workers begin to demand higher wages, forcing companies to make more money to pay those higher wages, keeping them profitable.

Here’s the catch: Inflation kills the value of the money in your savings account. Since prices keep rising, the purchasing power of the money you keep decreases.

What is Investing?

Think of investing as a way of making your money make money. Investing is putting your resources into an asset with the expectation that the value of this asset will appreciate over time.

The question now becomes, what is this asset? There are many types of assets that you can invest in. These are called “securities”. Securities are tradable financial contracts issued by companies and governments to other entities. They do this in order to raise capital (which you provide with your finances). They are traded at a verified and secure marketplace called the stock market.

The general process of investing is: you buy a security, its value grows, and you sell it later, but we will get more specific in the following sections.

Types of Securities

A stock is the most common type of security you may have heard of. A stock is like a piece of a company. They’re also called shares since you get a “share” of the company’s ownership. For this reason, stocks are also called equities. The most common type of stocks people buy are dividend-paying ones. A dividend is a payout that companies give to their shareholders (people who hold shares) once they make enough profit to do so. It goes without saying, but the more shares you own the more you end up making. This is considered the easiest way to get a return on your investments. Some stocks do not pay dividends, but you still own a piece of the company. This means they still have value and you can take loans against them. However, it's not all sunshine and rainbows: stocks are a high-risk investment. If the company goes under, so do your investments. Reward varies based on the company and how much you have invested in it.

A bond is another security you can own, though it is haram with a halal alternative. When you purchase a bond, you’re essentially giving an organisation a loan. This comes with two things: an interest rate from which you earn “coupon payments,” and a maturity date, which is when your money will be paid back to you. The halal alternative to bonds is sukuk, which replaces the coupon payments with a share of the profit of the organisation.

Consider a situation where there is a group of investors, none of whom can or are willing to buy a stock, want to spread the risk of their investments across multiple businesses. What should they do? Well, they pool their money into what is called a mutual fund. Then, they hire a professional portfolio manager to use this money to buy stocks across multiple assets, such as stocks and sukuk. You can buy shares of a mutual fund to, once again, spread the risk of your investments, with a small fee being paid to the professional help. A sub-type of mutual funds is Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs), which are like mutual funds, but they are actively traded in the stock market. Mutual funds can only be sold once per day: at the time the stock market closes and at a price called their Net Asset Value (NAV). However, if a mutual fund involves haram businesses, it is considered haram to invest in. Hence, it is best to find a Shariah-compliant mutual fund such as the S&P 500 Shariah Index.

Treasury bills (or T-bills) are very similar to bonds, only T-bills are backed by the government. This makes them a generally safer investment. If you buy a T-bill from a government, then they will return your investment to you (generally in a short time) with interest. This is, of course, haram. Halal alternatives exist, once again, being sukuk.

Of course, there are many more ways of investing but here I’ve given you some of the most common ones as well as a framework for how to evaluate them and ensure they align with your values.

How to Actually Do It

Now that you know the fundamentals, how do you actually put this into practice? Firstly, you must open an account with a broker licensed by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SECP). A broker is simply a middleman between an investor and a company. Examples of reliable brokers include AKD Securities and Al Meezan Investments. Open an account with them and specify that it should be an Islamic account (if you wish to make halal investments). The account should generally be set up with “Ready Delivery Trading Only”

To use this account to trade, your account must be linked to the Central Depository Company, which holds digital shares. There are two types of accounts you can open, both of which have different requirements and features.

  • A Sahulat Sarmayakari Account is a simple, beginner-friendly account that only requires a valid CNIC and basic details to open. However, annual investments are barred at 800,000 to 1,000,000 PKR annually, though this range varies by broker, so check with your broker for the current limit.
  • A Standard Account requires more documentation such as proof of income, but there is no limit to how much you can invest annually.
  • Roshan Digital Accounts exist for international Pakistanis, but I won’t be going into too much detail on those here.

Once you’ve opened an account, I recommend learning more about investing or hiring a professional to manage your portfolio. The fact of the matter is that investing requires frequently reading the news and staying up to date on how the market conditions are impacting the businesses you are investing in.

If you wish to invest in a halal way, there are two more steps you can take. Filtering halal stocks can be done using tools like the KSE Meezan Index (Also called KMI-30) or the KMI All Shares Islamic Index. In the unfortunate scenario where you end up making an un-Islamic dividend (i.e. the company paid the dividend after making money by un-Islamic means), you can purify that dividend by donating the percentage of it that is impure to a charity. Please note that this does not count towards zakat, and you cannot receive anything in return.

This wraps up my introduction to investing. Of course, the topic is much deeper than this and it can get much more complicated as you dip your toes deeper into it. Keep learning as your portfolio grows and you’re sure to do well.

Best of luck with your investments!
